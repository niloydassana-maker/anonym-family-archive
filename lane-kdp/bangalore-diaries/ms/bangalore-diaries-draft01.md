# THE BANGALORE DIARIES
### *a techno-romance — 2004 to 2006*
### NILOY

*for the interviewee who escaped the interviewer, and for the year of the flood*

---

## PROLOGUE — THE MIRROR

There is a bathroom in Bangalore where a man looked up and did not find himself.

He found her face.

I am told that is the clinical hour. I am telling you the true one. The mirror only showed what I had become by standing: her eyes, her waiting, her letter-writing stare living behind my own lashes. I had spent two years being loved the way a test is passed. I had spent one July being unable to reach a dead man across a river of rain. The mirror added the numbers. Sum: me, minus me, equals her.

You cannot unsee your own face wearing the face of the one who followed you everywhere.

I went to the palace after that. Not a palace. A doctor who kept one.

The smell of the catheter.
The smell of the antiseptic.
And her.

Three smells, in that order, for the rest of my life. This book is the smell in the middle.

## I — THE QUESTION NO ONE WAS MEANT TO CLEAR — 2004

Bangalore did not shock me the way the postcards promised.

I was not a culture shock. The pubs, the language walls, the weather, the traffic — I passed all of it without turning a page. A boy from Silchar who had fed tigers was not going to be surprised by people.

The city hit me *technical-shock* full in the chest. BHEL. BEL. GE. The companies I had mailed my résumé to from the back of an autorickshaw, the temples whose gate numbers I had worshipped from outside — laid **open**. Not as brochures. Not as press visits. As *usual*. Their turbine halls, their defence electronics, their grids and alloys and control rooms — the full marvel, exposed, in the ordinary Tuesday hands of the people who worked there. Everyone's daily bread, my life's fever-dream, sitting in canteens that served it without ceremony.

I came from a state that wondered, into a city where wondering was not the point. This is the sentence the guides never print: for a certain kind of engineering animal, seeing the real machine — the *large* one, operating, unguarded — is not education. It is weather in the lungs.

That was the city that took me in 2004, and that is why what happened next was the only thing that could have. My first assignment was her architecture.

I read it the way a boy reads weather for the first time — with awe. It was larger than the problem it solved, cleaner than the design brief that spawned it, and somewhere inside its last module it stopped. Not sloppily. Honestly. And it was there, in the stopping, that I made the discovery I had not known I carried: I could see *through* systems. Not the code — the machine underneath it. I could feel them the way you feel a draft behind a wall. I knew where they were wounded before I could say why.

Masters show you the place they cannot reach. I found hers by heart.

She handed it to me to finish.

Say that slowly. The woman whose work stood above me — not under, not beside, above — took the one problem she could not solve and passed it, signed, across the desk to the new boy, as a colleague would not, as a teacher would not, as a judge would never. The assignment was the letter. I saw a woman I had walked in fearing, sign her own incompleteness over to me like a deed. I cleared her wall. I finished her architecture. And in the margin of the handover, in a signature that knew it was defacing itself, was written what the specification could not: the proof.

She fell forever. In every word of hers after that.

She chose love over intelligence.

I despised it.

Hear the arithmetic a boy makes at twenty-two when the woman whose ceiling he stood beneath dims that fire at his name: if her love costs her her standard, what is that love but a resignation — and I wanted the resignation of no one, least of all hers. I would rather she leave me the unsolvable module, and me unmade, and the world all ordinary, than take a prize that arrived discounted because the judge had fallen for the candidate. Too undeserving. That was the only word in the room, and nobody in it was the man I wanted to be.

So I did the only thing a boy with no syllabus can do.

I escaped.

## II — JULY — THE RIVER ATE THE ROAD

And then the sentence that has no page in any manual of the time:

I started feeling them all.

Not after the machines. With them. One by one, like switches finding their lamps — every plant, every panel, every substation humming three streets away. I had come knowing that I could feel the wounded module inside one architecture. The city, being innocent, had not warned me that feeling is a muscle. I had opened the door with BHEL. The machines had opened mine.

The membrane between a man and a machine is not in the machine. That is nobody's textbook line. What I could not yet say — what would take a palace, a clinic, a decade, and a lifetime of paper to say *backwards* against me — was simple in the body and unspeakable in the office: things speak, and I could hear them, and no one had given me a volume knob.

So I did what the young do. I turned it up on the machines I loved, and I walked past the ones in me.

July is where you find out what that costs. My father died on the eighteenth of July.

He left by his own hand. In that district they do not call it suicide, they call it an act — the word still, the hand busy. I did not see it. There is a worse sentence: I tried.

The Brahmaputra rose out of nowhere, the way water goes when it has been keeping a plan. The roads opened and shut. My bus turned back. The train died mid-journey in a town whose name I now cannot say without tasting mud. Three days. The flood held me three days in a waiting room of earth.

Three days is not long in a year. In a funeral, it is a lifetime with the lights off. By the time the water finished its business, their business was done.

I could not see him the last time.

Everything that came after — the mirror, the palace, the clinic — the doctors looked for the illness in the blood. The illness was in the timetable. My father had gone and I had been cancelled, delayed, the way a train is.

When the final rite happens without its eldest son, the ancestors do not hold it against the son. The house holds it against you forever.

I went back to Bangalore to not be there. And Bangalore had one door left open.

## III — IMMERSION

I tried to escape the grief by immersing in her.

There is arithmetic to that, if you let me be clinical once: a drowning man grabbed the nearest hand and called it medicine, and medicine it was — it stopped the bleeding, not the wound. Julie — yes, she answered to her name but in the ledger of this book I allow her only the one I invented, which is truer than her real one in the way every beloved thing has a private name — Julie's love was the only object in the world that had already seen me at my highest and still wanted me at my ordinary. After a dead father and a washed road, that is not romance. That is gravity.

I went into her the way you go into a river when the bank is on fire.

For months it worked. Grief cannot touch a man fully occupied by another person's gladness. Her stare stopped being office policy and became weather I lived in. I stopped asking what July was for. I stopped sleeping badly. An interviewee who had escaped one woman let herself interview him back, and this time he passed both ways.

And then the medicine stopped.

It did not stop at a scene or a quarrel. It stopped the way a tourniquet stops. The grief had only been parked, not buried — the flood had stolen the burial, and parked grief comes back with a tow truck. I woke up in the bathroom. The face in the mirror was hers, and not hers, and finally no one's, and when I could not find my own among them all, I made a small sound you would not recognise as language and a large one you would not recognise as a man.

We went crazy. I tried to escape. I could not.

## IV — NOTHING FOUND, THEN EVERYTHING

First they tested the body. The hospital on Bannerghatta Road drew blood the way the police draw conclusions and handed it back clean. Endocrine department — diagnosis — nothing found.

*Nothing found* is the loneliest sentence in medicine. It means: your catastrophe is real and you are not, by any instrument of ours, ill. It means: go be mad somewhere else.

But her face was following me. Everywhere. In everything. In the steam of the tea at the canteen. In the margins of every assignment, still. In the blank before a monitor wakes — a rectangle of bathroom mirror, on every desk in that campus, following me out of the door and into the trains.

So we went to the palace.

The doctor at the palace had the furniture of a bygone kingdom and the patience of a man who has heard every sentence the mind can say about itself. He asked questions that the flood had never asked. He wrote down the word that I had been circling since July and I did not ask him, because I was not sure the asking and the answer were different things.

The first time I was diagnosed.

Life was never the same again. It is a cliché and clichés are scar tissue: they form over the exact shape of the wound. The word sat on me the way an anesthetic sits — what it numbs was never the pain that brought you in.

## V — THE YEAR WITH TWO DOCTORS — 2005

In 2005 the clinic gave me a name — the name for the blood — for what had come down my father's line, and what the whole world later learned to shout at me in papers, as if an inheritance were an accusation: *unfit.*

In 2005, also, my son was born.

Two doctors that year. One held a label and wrote me a sentence. The other was a mother's palm and a seven-pound fact and he wrote me the only answer any of this has ever had. The clinic said: *this is what came down the line.* My son said, by arriving: *and this is what keeps going down it.* You want to call that a disease? Watch him at six months. Watch him reach.

The label stayed on me like a borrowed coat. The house wore it into court. The court wrote it down. The file followed me through cities the way her face did, and both of them, I finally admit, looked like me by then.

The smell of the catheter.
The smell of the antiseptic.
And her.

I am writing this in a room where it is raining sideways, in a city with a river that once ate a road, with a name for her that she never knew I'd choose in the end — one that belongs to a play about two children who loved past every wall, past every family, past death itself. No one survived that play, but everyone remembers it. That is the entire plan.

You wanted forever, Julie.

Here it is: a book.
